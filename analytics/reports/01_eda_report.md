### The two strongest correlations

**1. `pclass` ↔ `fare` (r = -0.5482, |r| = 0.5482).** Ticket class and fare are two measurements of the same underlying thing — how much a passenger paid. `pclass` is coded 1/2/3 with 1 as the most expensive, so the relationship is negative by construction: as the class number rises the fare falls. The fact that it is strong but not near −1 tells us fare varied a lot *within* a class (cabin, port, family bookings).

**2. `sibsp` ↔ `parch` (r = +0.4145, |r| = 0.4145).** Siblings/spouses aboard and parents/children aboard move together because they are both slices of one latent variable: family group size. A passenger travelling as a family tends to register on both counters at once, and a solo traveller on neither — which is exactly why the derived `alone` flag was excluded from this matrix as redundant.

Both of the top pairs are *structural* rather than causal, which is the practical takeaway for modelling: they warn that the feature set carries redundancy, so a linear model's coefficients on these columns should not be read as independent effects.

### Chart 3 — age distribution by outcome

![chart3](figures/story_3_age_survival.png)

Age is a weak *linear* predictor but a real *threshold* one. Across the adult range the survived/not-survived split is roughly constant, which is why `age` barely registers in the correlation matrix. At the young end it flips: under-16s survived at 59.0% against 36.1% for everyone else. A linear model will underuse this; a tree, which can cut at a threshold, will not — and that is one reason to expect the tree-based models to edge ahead.

### Chart 4 — survival rate by family size

![chart4](figures/story_4_family_size.png)

Family size is non-monotonic, which is why neither `sibsp` nor `parch` shows much correlation on its own. Solo travellers survived at 30.1%; small families of 2–4 did best at 57.9%; large families of 5+ collapsed to 16.1%. The plausible mechanism is that a small group could be shepherded into a boat together, while a large group could not be kept together and would not board separately. The engineering lesson is that the useful feature here is a *bucketed* family size, not either raw counter.

### Chart 5 — pair plot of the six numeric features

![chart5](figures/story_5_pairplot.png)

Read as a whole, the pair plot shows no single numeric feature cleanly separates the two classes — the orange and blue clouds overlap everywhere. Separation only appears in combination (low `pclass` *and* high `fare`, or low `age` *and* small family), which is the argument for a non-linear model and against expecting much from any one-variable rule.

**Story in one line.** Survival was decided first by sex, then by class/deck position, with a genuine child bonus and a small-family bonus layered on top — and the numeric features encode these effects redundantly rather than independently.
